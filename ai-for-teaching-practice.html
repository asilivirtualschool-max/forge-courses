Adapting, diagnosing and researching your own classroom
Four modules · 6 hours · self-paced · portfolio-assessed · mapped to the UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Teachers (2024)

Before you start

Modules 1 and 2 can be done any time. Modules 3 and 4 need real student work and your own notes — do not run them dry.

This course is not about tools. Tools change every few months and a course built on them is out of date before it is delivered. It is about judgement: knowing which parts of your work AI can genuinely take, which parts it cannot, and how to tell the difference when the output looks confident either way.

You will finish able to adapt an existing resource to a different class without losing what made it work, read a set of student responses for the misconception underneath them, and write up your own classroom inquiry in a form another teacher could use.

Who it is for

  • Classroom teachers in any subject, primary or secondary.
  • Teachers who have tried AI, found the output plausible but somehow unusable, and stopped.
  • Teachers who have not tried it and would rather start with the limits than the hype.

No prior experience is assumed. Bring one resource you already use and one set of student work — you will work on your own material throughout.

Module 1 — What AI is actually good at

90 minutes · Outcome: you can say which parts of your own workload AI should touch, and defend the boundary.

1.1 The honest picture

Generative AI produces text that is plausible given everything it has read. That is not the same as text that is true, and it is not the same as text that is right for your class. It has no knowledge of your students, no access to what happened in your room on Tuesday, and no way of telling you when it is wrong.

Everything in this course follows from taking that seriously rather than working around it.

1.2 Where it earns its place, and where it does not

Use it forDo not use it for
Adapting something that already works — reading level, context, language demandJudging an individual student. It does not know them and cannot be accountable
Producing variations: parallel questions, worked examples, distractorsAnything where being confidently wrong is expensive — facts you will not check, references you will not read
Noticing patterns across a set of anonymised responsesDeciding what to teach. That is a curriculum judgement and it is yours
Turning your own rough notes into prose you can shareReplacing the thinking that planning does for you

1.3 The non-negotiables

Say it out loud, then keep saying it. No student names. No identifiable student work. No photographed exit tickets. Aggregate counts and anonymised quotes only. One breach ends both the research approval and, potentially, your standing. This rule returns at the start of Modules 3 and 4 because that is where the temptation arrives.

Activity — audit your week

Ten minutes alone, ten with a colleague.

  1. List everything you did for work last week.
  2. Mark each item: AI-suitable, AI-assisted, or AI-never.
  3. Compare with a partner and argue about three disagreements.
  4. Name one task you will try before Module 2.
What usually happens. Most teachers move at least one item from AI-never to AI-assisted, and at least one the other way. The argument is the learning — the list itself matters less.

Check your understanding

Which of these is the strongest reason not to use AI to identify struggling students?

A colleague says "I asked it for a scheme of work and it gave me a really good one." What is the most useful reply?

Name one task in your own week that you moved from AI-never to AI-assisted, and say what changed your mind.

A strong answer names a specific task, not a category, and identifies what made it safe: usually that the output would be checked by someone who knows the students, that no student data is involved, and that being wrong would be visible and cheap. A weak answer says "admin" — that is a category, and it hides the tasks within it where confident wrongness would actually cost something.

Module 2 — Adapting what already works

90 minutes · Outcome: you can reshape a resource for a different class without losing what made it work.

2.1 Why adaptation beats generation

Asking for a worksheet from nothing gets you something plausible and untested. Starting from a resource that has already worked in a real classroom and reshaping it keeps the part that was hard to get right — the sequence, the worked example, the choice of what to confront — and changes only the surface.

This is not only an argument about AI. It is how good departments have always worked.

2.2 The three adaptations worth knowing

AdaptationWhat it doesWhat goes wrong
Reading levelSame task, same cognitive demand, different linguistic loadLowering the thinking along with the language. This is the classic error — watch for it
ContextSame idea, a setting your students recogniseLosing the feature that made the original context work. Keep the original for comparison
Language demandAdding or removing sentence frames and vocabulary supportRemoving the requirement to explain, which removes your evidence

2.3 Give it the constraint, not the task

"Make this easier" produces mush.

A constraint that works: "Keep every step and the worked example exactly as they are; rewrite only the wording so a student reading two years below age level can follow it; do not remove the requirement to explain the reasoning." The constraint is the instruction.

2.4 The rejection habit

The skill in this module is not prompting. It is looking at a fluent, confident output and being able to say precisely what is wrong with it.

Teachers who accept the first output produce worse material than they would have written themselves. Teachers who reject two and take the third produce better. A vague rejection — "not quite right" — teaches you nothing; the specific fault is the entire skill.

Activity — adapt your own

  1. Take the resource you brought. Produce three adaptations — one of each type.
  2. Reject at least one output and write one sentence naming the specific fault.
  3. Say what that fault would have cost in the classroom if you had used it.
This is portfolio item one. Write the rejection into Part A of the Portfolio tab as you go.

Check your understanding

Which rejection is strong enough to count?

Why keep the original alongside a context adaptation?

What is the difference between adapting a resource and generating one, in terms of what you can claim about it?

With an adapted resource you can say that the structure has worked with real students, and that you changed only the surface — so the claim carries over unless your change broke something. With a generated one you can say only that it reads plausibly. The evidence lives in the structure: the sequence, the worked example, the choice of what to confront. That is exactly the part generation invents and adaptation preserves.

Module 3 — Reading the evidence

90 minutes · Outcome: you can find the misconception under a set of responses and write a question that tests for it. Do not attempt this without real student responses in front of you.

3.1 What a set of responses contains

A pile of wrong answers is not noise. Wrong answers cluster, and the cluster tells you what students actually believe. The most common wrong answer is more informative than the correct one, because it names the thing you have to teach against.

3.2 The boundary, restated

On the set, never on the child. AI can tell you that fourteen responses share a misconception. It must never tell you what to think about the child who wrote one of them. That judgement is yours, it requires knowing them, and it is not delegable.

3.3 Working with anonymised data

  • Strip names before anything else. Number the responses instead.
  • Transcribe or summarise rather than photographing.
  • Ask for patterns and counts, not verdicts.
  • Ask it to argue against its own reading — the second answer is usually better than the first.

3.4 Writing the hinge question

Once you know the dominant misconception, write one question whose wrong option is that misconception.

The test. If a student holding the misconception can still answer correctly, the question is not testing what you think it is. Try it yourself: answer your own question while deliberately holding the wrong belief. If you get it right, rewrite it.

This is the single most useful thing in the course and it takes about four minutes once you can see the cluster.

Activity — from responses to a hinge

  1. Anonymise and number a real set of responses.
  2. Find the dominant misconception. Ask for the pattern, then ask for the case against it.
  3. Write a hinge question with that misconception as a distractor.
  4. Swap with a colleague and try to answer their question while holding the wrong belief.
  5. If either of you gets it right that way, rewrite.
This is portfolio item two. Write the analysis and the finished question into Part B of the Portfolio tab.

Check your understanding

Why is the most common wrong answer more useful than the correct one?

You ask AI to analyse an anonymised response set and it gives you a clear reading. What should you do next?

Your hinge question can be answered correctly by a student holding the misconception. What has gone wrong, and how do you fix it?

The question is testing the topic rather than the threshold — usually because the correct answer is reachable by a route that does not require the new idea, or because the distractor is a careless error rather than the actual misconception. Fix it by making the misconception the most attractive wrong option, and by choosing a case where the old belief and the new one give different answers. If the two beliefs agree on your example, the example cannot discriminate, however hard it looks.

Module 4 — Researching your own practice

90 minutes · Outcome: you can run and write up a small piece of classroom inquiry another teacher could act on. Needs a term of notes behind it.

4.1 Why teachers are well placed to do this

Most education research is done by people who are not in the room. Practitioner action research is not a lesser version of that — it answers a different question. Not "does this work on average" but "what happened when I did this, with these students, and what would I do differently".

The second question is the one that changes practice.

4.2 The weekly note

Five minutes, every Friday, three prompts: what surprised me, what did not work, what I would change. That is the whole method.

It is also the first habit to lapse. Without it there is nothing to write up in December. You will not reconstruct week three in November, however well you think you remember it.

4.3 Interrogating your own notes

  • Paste your own notes — they contain no student names if you have written them properly.
  • Ask for recurring themes, then ask what the notes contradict.
  • Ask it to make the case against your conclusion. Take that seriously.
  • Keep what you disagree with. Where you and the analysis differ is usually where the finding is.

4.4 Writing it up

Four paragraphs: what I was trying to find out, what I did, what happened, what I would do differently. Another teacher should be able to read it and act. Length is not a virtue here.

Activity — draft one week

  1. Write one week of notes from a real lesson you taught this year, using the three prompts.
  2. Turn it into a paragraph another teacher could act on.
  3. Ask for the case against your own conclusion, and decide what you make of it.
This is portfolio item three. Your five-hundred-word reflection goes into Part C of the Portfolio tab.

Check your understanding

What makes a practitioner action-research write-up useful to another teacher?

You have kept the weekly note for four of twelve weeks. What is the best response?

What is the difference between a reflection and a report of a successful intervention?

A reflection includes what you got wrong, what surprised you, and where you changed your mind — including conclusions you no longer hold. A report of success selects the evidence that supports the thing you already believed. The second is more comfortable to write and almost useless to read, because the reader cannot tell what was tested from what was assumed. If nothing in your write-up would embarrass you slightly, it is probably the second kind.
