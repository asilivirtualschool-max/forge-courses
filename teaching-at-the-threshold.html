Teaching at the Threshold

Troublesome knowledge, liminality and skill levels
Four modules · 6 hours · self-paced · portfolio-assessed

Before you start

Read this, then work through the modules in order. Modules 1 and 2 must be done in order; 3 and 4 can be swapped.

This course is not about a new initiative. It is about a question that sits underneath all planning and is rarely asked out loud: which idea in this unit is genuinely hard, and how would I know if a student had crossed it?

You will finish with one unit of your own fully planned through that lens, and the ability to tell the difference between a student who is stuck and a student who is mid-crossing — which is the single most useful diagnostic judgement in this whole field.

Who it is for

  • Classroom teachers in any subject, primary or secondary.
  • Curriculum leads and heads of department auditing schemes of work.
  • Instructional coaches and those running departmental professional learning.

No prior knowledge is assumed. Bring one unit you will teach next term — you will work on it throughout.

Module 1 — What makes knowledge troublesome

90 minutes · Outcome: you can identify a threshold concept in your own subject and defend the choice.

1.1 The problem with planning by coverage

Most planning starts from the topic and the activity. Everything gets taught, in order, and students complete tasks successfully. The trouble is that a student can complete every task correctly and still hold the misconception the unit was meant to overturn.

A threshold concept is different from ordinary content. It is the idea that, once grasped, changes how a student sees the whole subject. Meyer and Land introduced the term in 2003 to describe these portals — you cannot progress without passing through them, and once through, you cannot go back.

1.2 The five tests

Not everything hard is a threshold. Apply all five:

Transformative

It changes how the learner sees the subject, not just what they know. This is the decisive test — if it is not transformative, it is merely difficult content.

Irreversible

Once understood, it is very hard to un-know. This is why experts struggle to remember not knowing it.

Integrative

It ties together things that previously seemed unrelated.

Troublesome

It is counter-intuitive, or it conflicts with something the learner already believes and which has worked for them.

Discursive

Crossing it changes how the learner talks about the subject. This is why every threshold carries a language demand.

1.3 Perkins' five types of troublesome knowledge

David Perkins categorised why knowledge is troublesome. This matters because each type has a different fix — naming the type tells you what to do.

TypeWhat it looks likeClassroom exampleThe fix
RitualPerformed fluently, means nothingA pupil executes long multiplication perfectly and cannot say what it is forRebuild from a model that carries meaning, then re-connect the procedure
TacitThe expert move was never made visibleSelecting the right evidence for a literary claim; deciding which variable to controlThink aloud, model the invisible decision, then fade
InertKnown, but never used unpromptedDefines persuasive language in a test, reads the newspaper without noticing itPractise in varied, unsignalled contexts
Conceptually difficultRests on a genuinely hard ideaConservation of matter; density as a ratioConcept work alongside — drilling will not fix it
AlienA foreign way of seeingNegative quantity; light entering the eye; energy as a system propertyBridge from an intuition they already hold

Activity — audit your unit

Ten minutes alone, then ten with a colleague if you have one.

  1. List everything your unit covers. Now cross out anything merely factual or procedural.
  2. From what remains, choose the ONE idea that, if a student truly got it, would change how they see the subject.
  3. Test it against all five criteria. Write one sentence for each.
  4. If it fails 'transformative', choose again. Most first attempts do.
  5. Now name WHY it is troublesome using Perkins' five types.
Expect to fail twice. The commonest error is choosing something hard but not transformative — 'long division', 'the water cycle'. Push for the underlying idea: place-value decomposition, conservation of matter. That struggle is the learning.

Check your understanding

Which of the five tests most reliably separates a threshold concept from merely difficult content?

A student carries out a procedure flawlessly but cannot explain any step. Which type of troublesome knowledge is this?

Why does the 'discursive' criterion mean every threshold concept carries a language demand?

Because crossing changes how a learner talks, not only what they can do. New relationships need new grammar — comparatives, conditionals, causal connectives — and vocabulary that names the relationship rather than the object. If you do not teach that language explicitly, students who have crossed cannot show you, and students who have not can borrow the words and appear to have crossed. The language is evidence as well as content.

Module 2 — Liminality: the space between

90 minutes · Outcome: you can distinguish a student who is stuck from one who is mid-crossing, and respond differently to each.

2.1 What liminality is

Liminality comes from the Latin limen, a threshold or doorframe. It names the space a learner occupies once they have left their old understanding behind but have not yet arrived at the new one. It is not a failure state. It is what crossing actually feels like from the inside.

Meyer and Land describe learners in this space as oscillating between old and new understandings, showing both attempts at understanding and troubled misunderstanding — often in the same lesson.

2.2 The three phases

Pre-liminal

The learner has not yet encountered the idea as problematic. Their existing understanding still works for them and they see no reason to change it. Teaching here is about creating the need.

Liminal

The old understanding has been disturbed but the new one is not secure. Expect oscillation, frustration, mimicry of the right language without the understanding behind it, and sometimes real distress.

Post-liminal

The idea is held and used. The learner talks differently about the subject. Crucially they now find it hard to remember not understanding it.

2.3 Mimicry — the trap

A learner in the liminal space often produces language that sounds like understanding. They use the right vocabulary in roughly the right places. Meyer and Land call this mimicry, and it is the reason task completion is such a poor measure of crossing.

The test is not whether the words appear. It is whether the learner can apply the idea in a case they have not been taught, and explain why.

2.4 The emotional dimension

Crossing a threshold can be uncomfortable. The learner is being asked to give up something that worked. In some subjects it touches identity — the student who was "good at maths" because they were fast at procedures now finds that fluency is not enough.

This has a direct teaching consequence: naming the difficulty out loud helps. Telling a class that this idea is genuinely hard, that being confused is expected, and that it will resolve, is not lowering expectations. It is accurate, and it reduces the chance that a student concludes they are simply not clever.

2.5 Stuck or liminal? The judgement that matters

StuckLiminalWhat to do
PatternConsistently wrong, in the same wayRight one day, wrong the next; oscillatingStuck: diagnose. Liminal: hold.
LanguageUses old language confidentlyReaches for new language, uses it impreciselyLiminal is a good sign, not a bad one
EmotionOften untroubled — they think they are rightFrustrated, uncertain, may say it 'used to make sense'Name it: this is expected
ResponseRe-teach at the broken sub-skillTime, repeated encounters, keep scaffolds availableDo NOT intervene harder on a liminal student
The counter-intuitive point. The instinct with an oscillating, frustrated student is to intervene harder — more practice, a lower group, an intervention slot. That is precisely wrong. Oscillation is the documented signature of crossing. It resolves with time and repeated encounters, and intervening harder can convert a normal liminal wobble into a belief that they cannot do the subject.

Activity — liminality spotting

For each of the following, decide: pre-liminal, liminal, post-liminal, or stuck — and justify it before you check.

  1. A pupil writes '0.45 is bigger than 0.5 because 45 is bigger than 5' and is confident.
  2. A pupil says 'the mass stays the same… no wait, it gets lighter because the gas went… I don't know any more.'
  3. A pupil uses the word 'symbolises' correctly in an essay but cannot say what evidence supports it.
  4. A pupil spontaneously asks whether a text they are reading for fun is trying to persuade them.
  5. A pupil who was fluent last week now hesitates and asks 'but why does that work?'
Answers. 1 — pre-liminal (no disturbance yet). 2 — liminal (oscillating, distressed). 3 — liminal, and specifically mimicry. 4 — post-liminal (transfer, unprompted). 5 — liminal, and a positive sign: hesitation after fluency often marks the start of real understanding.

Check your understanding

A student's work gets worse after a lesson. Which reading is most likely to be right?

Which observation best distinguishes mimicry from genuine crossing?

Why is 'intervene harder' the wrong response to a liminal student?

Because the intervention treats a normal, temporary state as a deficit. Oscillation is the signature of crossing, and it resolves with time and repeated encounters rather than with more practice. Intervening harder does three things wrong: it practises at the top of a chain that is not broken, it removes the time the student needs, and it signals to the student that their confusion is a problem with them. That last one is how a liminal wobble becomes a settled belief that they cannot do the subject.

Module 3 — Skill levels and progression

90 minutes · Outcome: you can name the skill a threshold demands, chain its prerequisites, and locate where a learner actually broke down.

3.1 Concepts and skills fail differently

A concept is troublesome because it is counter-intuitive. A skill is troublesome for different reasons — most often because it can be performed correctly with no understanding at all, or because the expert move behind it was never made visible.

Every threshold concept demands a skill. You cannot cross the place-value threshold without composing and decomposing numbers flexibly. You cannot cross the symbolism threshold without building an evidenced interpretive claim. Naming that skill is what makes the concept teachable.

3.2 Two kinds of skill, kept apart

Discipline-specific procedural skill

The concrete moves: partition a whole into equal parts; construct a fair test; cite textual evidence. This is what you teach.

Transferable capability

What it develops underneath: proportional reasoning, modelling, argumentation. This is what you bank across the curriculum. Anchor it to a published framework — ACER's five skills, or the IB ATL clusters — so it is more than a phrase.

3.3 The sub-skill chain — diagnosing backwards

Most skill failure is not failure of the target skill. It is a missing link further back. The chain for comparing decimals runs: whole-number place value → tenths → hundredths → equivalence → comparison. A pupil failing the last step has usually broken at the second.

The diagnostic move is to run the chain backwards, asking the pupil to do each link, and find the first one that fails. Then teach THERE, not at the target.

Why this matters. Practice at the top of a broken chain compounds confusion. It is the single commonest waste of intervention time.

3.4 Proficiency levels

A skill is not present or absent — it develops. Both the IB ATL framework and ACER's skill development frameworks describe levels with observable indicators. A workable four-level scale:

LevelWhat it looks likeTypical bandYour response
NoviceCan do it with the model in front of themK–2Keep the model visible
LearnerCan do it with prompting or in the taught context3–5Fade the prompt; change the context
PractitionerDoes it independently in familiar contexts6–8Vary the context; increase complexity
ExpertApplies it in unfamiliar contexts, unprompted9–12Ask them to critique someone else's use of it

Read the level as the target for that year, not a judgement of the child.

3.5 Mastery is not task completion

Define mastery as an observable behaviour, not a finished piece of work. 'Regroups without prompting when it helps' is a mastery criterion. 'Completes the worksheet' is not.

The test for any success criterion. Could a student complete this successfully while still holding the misconception? If yes, it measures the task, not the threshold.

Activity — build and break a chain

  1. Take the threshold you identified in Module 1. Name the ONE skill it demands, as a verb.
  2. Write the sub-skill chain — the ordered prerequisites, four to six links.
  3. Swap with a partner. Their job is to find the link most of your class would break at.
  4. Write the observable mastery criterion for the target skill. Apply the test above.
  5. Assign a target proficiency level for your year group.

Check your understanding

A pupil fails a skill that has been taught well. What is the most likely explanation?

Rewrite this as a proper mastery criterion: 'Students will understand equivalent fractions.'

Something like: 'Generates an equivalent fraction for a given fraction without prompting, and explains why the two name the same quantity, using a representation of their choice.' It names an observable behaviour, it specifies unprompted independence, and — the crucial part — a student who still believes 2/4 is a different number from 1/2 could not satisfy it. Apply that test to any criterion you write: could a student holding the misconception still tick this box?

Why is 'approaching' a more useful judgement than 'nearly there'?

Because 'approaching' names a position on a described continuum with observable indicators, and 'nearly there' names a feeling about distance from an unstated standard. 'Approaching' tells you and the student what the next observable behaviour is; 'nearly there' tells them only that they have not arrived. The first is actionable, the second is reassurance.

Module 4 — Teaching across the threshold

90 minutes · Outcome: you can plan a sequence that moves a class through a misconception, build the four documents that carry it, and sort the evidence four ways rather than two.

4.1 The sequence

Overcoming a misconception is a different instructional problem from teaching a new idea. The research-supported sequence has five steps, and the order matters more than any single step.

StepMoveWhat you doWhy here
1ElicitSurface the belief — hinge question, concept cartoon, written predictionYou cannot change an idea the student has not voiced
2BridgeAnchor on a case where their intuition is ALREADY rightThe step most often skipped — see below
3ConfrontShow the contradiction — demonstration, measurement, dataOnly now does the anomaly land
4ResolveTeach the correct model and apply it to structured examplesConflict alone does not build the new idea
5ReinforcePractise on confusable cases; revisit laterThe old idea was useful for years; one lesson does not undo it
Bridge before you confront. Confront first and students explain the anomaly away, distrust the demonstration, or lose confidence. Anchor first on a case where they are already right — money for decimals ($0.45 versus $0.50), sharing a pizza for fractions, a team for ecosystems — then move to the hard case.

The Sequence Map is where this lives: one page per unit, one row per lesson, with the move named before any activity is chosen. The crossing is rarely lesson one, because eliciting and bridging have to happen first.

4.2 The four documents that carry it

A sequence that exists only in your head does not survive a week of cover, illness and a fire drill. Four documents carry it, and each answers a question the one above it cannot.

  • Sequence Map — the whole unit on one page. How many lessons, in what order, and which one carries the crossing. Completed once, before you teach anything.
  • Lesson Plan — one lesson. Declare the move at the top before writing any activity. That single step is what stops activity-first planning.
  • Worksheet — what students do. Retrieval, then a worked example, then a completion task with the last steps removed, then independent work. The order is the design, not a suggestion.
  • Exit Ticket — three to five minutes at the end. Two-tier: the answer, and the reason for it. This is where your evidence comes from.

Build them in that order. A worksheet written before the sequence is settled will be a good activity attached to the wrong lesson.

4.3 Diagnostic question formats

Five formats, each revealing something different. Distractors should come from documented misconceptions, not invented wrong answers.

  • Simple multiple choice — fast whole-class check.
  • Two-tier — answer plus reason. The second tier exposes the misconception itself.
  • Confidence grid — right/wrong plus how sure. Separates a secure misconception from a guess, which need different teaching.
  • Talking heads or concept cartoon — characters voice rival views, so the wrong idea belongs to a character and is safe to defend.
  • Explanation story — students choose the scientifically correct wording at several points. Targets language directly.

4.4 Reading the evidence — the four piles

Decide in advance what you will accept as evidence. Not "completed the task" but the specific behaviour: compares decimals by place value rather than digit count; predicts that the water level will not change. Then sort each set of responses into four piles rather than two. The pile sizes are your plan for the next lesson.

PileWhat it looks likeWhat you do next
CrossedRight answer, the reason is their own, transfer item right tooMove them on; do not re-teach
LiminalRight then wrong, or right but hedged; reverts on transferMore time in the same phase; another representation, not another explanation
MimickingRight answer, your words back at you, transfer item wrongThe dangerous pile — it looks like success. Ask them to explain it to someone who believes the old thing
Pre-liminalWrong answer, confidently, old reasoning intactThe confrontation has not happened for them yet
Why the second tier is not optional. Right-and-wrong marking cannot see the mimicking pile. Then re-check six to eight weeks later: a threshold genuinely crossed holds, and a drop means it was performance rather than understanding.

Activity — build the sequence

  1. For your threshold, complete a Sequence Map: six lessons, with the move declared for each one before you choose any activity.
  2. Write the eliciting question you will open with, and identify the bridging anchor — where is your class already right about something structurally similar?
  3. Choose the confrontation. What will they see that they cannot explain away?
  4. Draft the exit ticket for the crossing lesson: a two-tier question whose wrong option is your named misconception, plus one transfer item set in a context you have not taught.
  5. Write the signs of crossing in one observable sentence, and plan the re-check — which question, how many weeks later?

Check your understanding

Why does confronting before bridging often fail?

Which of the four piles is invisible to right-and-wrong marking?

Why re-check six weeks later rather than at the end of the unit?

Because an end-of-unit check measures performance while the teaching is still fresh, and performance decays in a way that understanding does not. A threshold genuinely crossed holds up weeks later without revision — that is what irreversibility means in practice. A drop at six weeks tells you the class produced the right behaviour under recent instruction rather than acquiring the idea, which is exactly the distinction the whole course is about. It also catches mimicry, which is very durable across a week and very fragile across a term.
